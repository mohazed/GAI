Rediscovering the Mediterranean Horizon
At the beginning of one of Jorge Luis
Borges’ celebrated short stories, it
occurs to the protagonist, Espinosa, that
“generations of men, throughout recorded
time, have always told and retold two
stories – that of a lost ship which searches
the Mediterranean seas for a dearly loved
island, and that of a god who is crucified on
Golgotha.”
One may quibble with Espinosa regarding
the exhaustiveness of his list. Nevertheless,
it is hard to dispute that Homer’s Odyssey
is a fundamental, unconscious resource for
the human imagination, which continues
to be drawn upon across many civilisations
– and the Mediterranean Sea is the basic
fabric of Homer’s narrative.
The speed with which it is possible to
traverse the globe in our contemporary – a
speed with which, in diplomatic work, we
are acutely familiar – makes it easy to forget
that, in Homer’s Greece, the Mediterranean
framed the known world, (almost) in its
entirety. It does not matter whether the
stops along Odysseus’s epic journey refer
to actually-existing places, or whether they
are interspersed with fantastical inventions;
the Mediterranean is that horizon of wonder
from which Homer draws the setting of his
epic adventure.
To reverse Espinosa’s musing, this issue
of
Parisio has sought to search
for the
Mediterranean
from our island. I have
frequently expressed the view that, too
often, the Mediterranean is rendered
invisible by an engagement that concerns
itself with a basket of individual dossiers
– the migration question, the security
question, the fragmentation into North and
South, East and West. The Mediterranean
Sea is not simply a conveyance from one
destination to another. It is a theatre in
which different facets of the universe
present themselves, where each derives
meaning from its framing in the whole. It
is my belief that, if we are to understand
the pressing questions of today’s
Mediterranean, we must do so within a
unitary, Mediterranean horizon.
The appointment of a European
Commissioner for the Mediterranean, at the
urging of Malta and other European Union
member states from the Mediterranean,
was an important step towards this re-
conception of Mediterranean questions
within the EU.
In her contribution, Commissioner
Dubravka Šuica, the first for the
Mediterranean, goes to lengths to
emphasize the spirit of joint ownership
which must suffuse initiatives such as the
New Pact for the Mediterranean.
Similarly, H.E. Ambassador Nasser Kamel,
Secretary General of the Union for the
Mediterranean, discusses the need for
a shared Mediterranean infrastructure,
physical and political, which is fit for the
needs of our time whilst remaining faithful to
the Barcelona Declaration of 30 years ago.
Finally, H.E. Ghassan Salamé, formerly UN
SRSG for Libya and now Minister of Culture
of Lebanon, reminds us that it was the
Mediterranean’s networks that ultimately
sustained it across millennia. The small,
he exhorts, must rediscover how to work
together.
I wish you a pleasant reading of this issue
of
Parisio.
Christopher Cutajar

Editor PARISIO
Pax Mediterranea: an optimist’s ideal?
The Mediterranean region remains
in seeming inertia. Politically, as
etymologically, it is defined by the in-
between. Significant political and social
initiatives have been undertaken. However,
it is arguable that tangible results remain
limited.
This November marks 30 years since the
Barcelona Euro-Mediterranean Conference
was convened, leading to the adoption
of the Barcelona Declaration and the
establishment of the Euro-Mediterranean
Partnership.
The Declaration affirmed a collective
resolve rooted in the conviction that:
‘the general objective of turning the
Mediterranean basin into an area of
dialogue, exchange and cooperation
guaranteeing peace, stability and prosperity
requires a strengthening of democracy
and respect for human rights, sustainable
and balanced economic and social
development, measures to combat poverty
and promotion of greater understanding
between cultures, which are all essential
aspects of partnership.’
A third of a century provides an
appropriate opportunity to evaluate
progress made. Has this stated conviction
been realised through demonstrable
political determination to fulfil the
promises of dialogue, cooperation, stability,
and prosperity, thus transforming the
Mediterranean into a zone of peace?
Is it accurate to claim that the region is
demonstrably better off than it was 30
years ago?
Current assessments suggest progress
has been uneven. There have been
significant developments, but the majority
of the Declaration’s original signatories
are no longer actively engaged, and the
political landscape has shifted considerably.
On the surface, the Mediterranean has
seen many changes, yet ongoing and
longstanding challenges remain deeply
anchored. The Barcelona Process evolved
into the Union for the Mediterranean, but
the extent of its impact remains subject
to debate. Beyond technical initiatives,
evidence of deep political headway is
limited. Some observers perceive ongoing
efforts as reiterative, echoing Alphonse
Karr’s observation:
Plus ça change, plus
c’est la même chose.
Shallow movement catches the eye but
leaves the depths – and the challenges
anchored therein – unperturbed. This
begs the question of what forces are
contributing to this persistent inertia.
Historically, attempts to advance regional
cooperation in the Mediterranean have
often become contingent on the Middle
East Peace Process. Yet it is worth
considering whether additional factors are
at play, possibly serving the interests of
external powers. In essence, the question
arises:
cui bono?
The notion that European and
Mediterranean security are interdependent
is widely accepted, though possibly to the
point of cliché. Surely, given its status and
its proximity, Europe’s influence over the
Mediterranean must be primordial.
Migration seems to be the EU’s main
political focus in the Mediterranean. This
is likely due to growing sensitivity to this
issue among European voters. As European
governments’ responses are heavily
shaped by public opinion, the EU loses its
margin of manoeuvre in the Mediterranean.
The risk is that such a migration-focused
approach may erode the credibility of the
European Union itself with its partners in
the Southern Neighbourhood. The pursuit
of self-interest is expected in foreign policy,
but it is unlikely to build many friendships
unless tempered by trust – and principles.
Accusations of inconsistency have
been levelled against the EU over its
approach to certain issues confronting
the Mediterranean region. On one side,
critics contend its response is insufficient
in certain contexts, such as its actions in
Yemen or in combating antisemitism. On
the other, there are claims the EU’s efforts
in the region are slower and less resolute
when compared to its engagement in other
global crises. This nuanced critique reflects
the complexity of expectations surrounding
European involvement and the persistent
debate over whether the EU is meeting its
responsibilities in the Mediterranean in a
timely and effective manner.
Europe’s response to issues concerning
its southern neighbours is often perceived
as timid, and its statements as lacking
strength and urgency, particularly regarding
issues related to international law – and
particularly in contrast to its (rightly)
vociferous defence of Ukraine since
February 2022.
It is not accurate to state that the
EU has taken no action regarding the
Mediterranean. The EU has appointed
a dedicated Commissioner for the
Mediterranean, Dubravka Šuica, and will
soon release its regional strategy. ‘The New
Pact for the Mediterranean’ will be aimed at
enhancing cooperation in the region more
than ever. There are many other European
initiatives, but they remain at the political
level and often do not receive significant
public attention they deserve.
It is therefore increasingly necessary to
examine whether the EU and its Southern
Mediterranean partners truly embody the
principle of security interdependence, or
whether external actors such as the United
States and the Russian Federation, exert
greater influence.
For instance, from the Middle East to
Libya, questions persist regarding the
identity of the primary actors driving
outcomes and prolonging instability. Other
regional concerns include the Cyprus issue,
Eastern Mediterranean disputes, Lebanon,
Syria, tensions concerning Western
Sahara, the South-South divide, climate
change, terrorism, migration, populism,
trafficking, and violations of international
law – a complex and multifaceted array
of challenges. The harder one looks,
the more it becomes apparent that the
Mediterranean is a Petri dish of competing
vested interests.
Geography is inescapable. The
Mediterranean occupies a central place
in Europe’s neighbourhood, with direct
repercussions on security and stability.
The effectiveness of European political
engagement is therefore crucial. The EU
needs to be motivated and ambitious to
strengthen and clarify its involvement.
Otherwise, there is a significant likelihood
that external powers will step in to fill any
resulting void, potentially shaping the
region’s trajectory in ways that may not
align with European interests.
Historically, the Mediterranean
experienced periods of relative peace
under the Roman Empire
(Pax Romana) and
later British dominance (
Pax Britannica),
when the region was considered a
strategic asset. Nowadays,
Pax Europea
remains a mirage and sovereignty rests
with independent Mediterranean states.
Thus, the concept of a
Pax Mediterranea
is now determined by regional actors and
their vision and political will. Whether this
is a realistic pursuit or an optimistic ideal,
warrants critical reflection.

Renewing our resolve for a peaceful, humane world
With the raising of the Maltese flag in
Place de la République in the heart of
Strasbourg, this year I proudly marked the
beginning of the 2025 Maltese Presidency
of the Council of Europe.
The symbolic ceremony officially
commenced the six-month political and
cultural programme of the fourth Maltese
Presidency of the Council of Europe. The
May-November 2025 Maltese Presidency,
coinciding with the 60th anniversary of
Malta’s membership in this organisation,
is prioritising the protection of children,
combatting violence and discrimination,
empowering youth and safeguarding
democracy and the rule of law.
To us Maltese, it has marked a new
chapter and a solemn pledge to defend the
values that unite us, to champion the rights
and dignity of all people, and to uphold the
mission of this great institution in word and
deed.
Malta has stepped into this role with a
deep sense of responsibility, to protect the
most vulnerable and uphold the values of
human rights, democracy, and the rule of
law.
And at a time when war continues to
inflict suffering on our continent, the
Council of Europe must remain a beacon
of justice, accountability, and democratic
resilience. Through this Presidency we
have continued to reinforce and promote
our conviction that principled, cooperative
diplomacy is the only path to lasting peace
and progress.
It had been only a few months since the
end of the 2024 Maltese chairpersonship
of the Organisation for Security and
Cooperation in Europe (OSCE), where in
returning to the OSCE Permanent Council
in June, I reaffirmed Malta’s steadfast
commitment to multilateralism and
underscored the crucial role of the Council
of Europe and the OSCE in promoting
peace, security and cooperation.
This is a decisive moment for Europe,
as existing and new challenges continue
to pose serious threats to multilateralism
and the values of democracy, peace and
respect for human rights. We cannot
remain complacent. We need to redouble
our efforts to ensure that these values
prevail.
Our collective achievements in 2024
demonstrate that despite the growing
geopolitical pressures we face, consensus
can still be reached with a constructive
spirit, the belief in diplomacy, and political
will.
Malta will always encourage Europe to
resist the temptation of accepting the
suffering of ongoing conflicts as inevitable.
Normalising conflict undermines our
shared humanity and erodes the very
principles on which peace and justice
are built. Instead, it is our collective
responsibility to uphold those principles
and to continue working together toward
peaceful and lasting solutions.
Malta has kept Ukraine at the top of
the Council of Europe’s agenda, steering
forward the establishment of the Council
of Europe’s Special Tribunal for the
Crime of Aggression against Ukraine.
By supporting this Tribunal, the Council
of Europe and its member states are
demonstrating their willingness to deliver
justice, not just words.
Behind every conflict, there are families,
children and communities whose daily
realities are shaped by fear, loss and
uncertainty. We must never lose sight of
the deep and lasting toll these conflicts
take on the people, whose only fate was to
be born in the crossfire.
Malta continues to express support
efforts towards a full, unconditional 30-day
ceasefire in Ukraine, to reduce civilian
suffering and to allow space for meaningful
talks toward a genuine peace.
The same has to be said of the grave
situation in the Middle East; while the
Council of Europe has unequivocally
condemned the attacks carried out by
Hamas on 7 October 2023, we remain
equally appalled by the suffering that
ensued and is still ongoing, in Gaza.
I am compelled to stress once again that
the use of starvation as a weapon of war
and forced mass displacement are serious
violations of international law, including
international humanitarian law. Malta’s
deep concern over all these actions is
that they undermine the overarching goal
of the Two-State solution. As a staunch
advocate for fundamental human rights,
Malta has supported a Netherlands call for
the EU to review Article 2 of the EU-Israel
Association Agreement.
Over the past five years, Malta allocated
more than €1.1 million in assistance to
Palestine. This includes annual financial
contributions to the United Nations
Relief and Works Agency for Palestine
Refugees in the Near East (UNRWA),
as well as donations to UNICEF, World
Central Kitchen, and other international
organisations that support Palestinian
communities. Malta also continues to
offer University of Malta scholarships to
Palestinian students.
In June, Malta sent 250 tonnes of flour
to the people of Gaza. These 10,000
sacks of flour were shipped to Limassol,
in collaboration with the Government of
Cyprus and the United Nations Office
for Project Services (UNOPS), to be
distributed to civilians by the international
organisation World Central Kitchen.
As always, we have continued to call
for de-escalation, an immediate end to
hostilities, and the unconditional release
of all 7th October hostages. Dialogue and
international collaboration remain the only
viable path to lasting peace and stability in
the Middle East.
Another immediate concern in our
Mediterranean neighbourhood has been
the creation of lasting stability in Libya.
We will always stand ready to contribute
to a renewed EU and UN effort in Libya,
towards unified institutions and lasting
stability. Recent escalations served as a
stark reminder of the untenability of fragile
states, the underlying divisions in Libya,
and the risk of renewed conflict. Malta has
always maintained that real, sustainable
progress depends on a genuinely inclusive,
Libyan-owned process. However,
continued international engagement
remains essential, particularly to safeguard
Libya’s sovereignty, ensure full respect
for the arms embargo and advance the
withdrawal of foreign forces.
Genuine political dialogue amongst
stakeholders on the ground is essential.
The process must remain Libyan-led and
Libyan-owned. We continue to support
proactive engagement by the international
community in support of UN-led efforts
– avoiding a political vacuum that could
be exploited by other malign actors is
essential.
In this spirit, we look ahead with resolve
to shape a more peaceful, just, and
humane world together.
Hon. Ian Borg
Deputy Prime Minister and Minister for

beauty, but shadowed by geopolitical conflict, environmental
exploitation, and migration crises. This crowded, energetic
composition embodies the Mediterranean paradox: paradise
edged with peril, where the celebration of life coexists with the
undercurrents of foreign interests, and unresolved tensions.

In 2024, the Malta Airport Foundation marked a significant
milestone, as it celebrated a decade of championing the
cultural, natural, and historical heritage that defines the

Maltese Islands, that watched the country’s rich history unfold,
serve as physical links to defining moments of our past that

well-maintained sites which have been given a new lease of life

Preserving Malta’s Artistic Heritage
As religious heritage forms an intrinsic part of Malta’s
identity, the Malta Airport Foundation has also been
instrumental in the preservation of sacred art. The
restoration of important religious artworks in Ta’

Until recently, peace looked like an illusion.

For centuries, the Mediterranean has
been both a cradle of civilizations and
a meeting point of continents. It has also
been a mirror of the world’s divisions. Today,
it stands as a line between a northern shore
where prosperity and institutional stability
largely prevail, and a southern shore marked
by economic fragility and political unrest.
This divide reflects a widening asymmetry of
development, governance, and power across
the sea.
In this landscape, the smallest of its coastal
states (Malta, Lebanon, Cyprus, Montenegro,
and Tunisia) bear burdens that exceed their
size. They did not spark the storms that now
sweep the region, yet must daily confront
the tides of migration, financial strain, and
environmental pressure stirred by conflicts
beyond their control. In a basin that birthed
both empires and exiles, they are too small
to set the rules, yet too exposed to ignore the
disorder.
The protective cushion of external shelter
has worn thin. Western capitals, preoccupied
by domestic division, the return of war on
European soil, and mounting debt burdens,
have reduced their outward assistance.
Other regional powers have stepped into
the gap, but their logic tends toward strategy
rather than institution-building. Their
interests may bring investment, but rarely
the structural reinforcement that small
Mediterranean states need.
Multilateral organisations such as the
European Union, the World Bank, or the
United Nations, remain active, yet their
resources are overstretched. According to the
United Nations Office for the Coordination
of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA), by May
2024 the total confirmed funding for the 2024
Global Humanitarian Overview had declined
by approximately 20 % than in 2023.¹
Crises around the Mediterranean no longer
stop at its shores, entangling global energy
routes, trade flows, and security balances
far beyond the region. The war in Ukraine
has reshaped energy and grain corridors
that directly influence food security from
the Maghreb to the Levant. The UN FAO
estimated that wheat-import costs for Arab
Mediterranean states rose by more than 40%
in 2022.² Meanwhile, renewed conflict in
Gaza and southern Lebanon has disrupted
regional trade and political stability, and the
long-running instability of Libya and Syria
continues to spur migration, arms flows, and
economic disruption.
Small states sit at the intersection of these
upheavals. Lebanon, with around 5.5 million
inhabitants, still hosts more than one million
displaced Syrians, one of the highest refugee-
to-population ratio in the world.³ Malta,
though far smaller, faces relentless migratory
pressure across the central Mediterranean:
Frontex registered nearly 17,000 irregular
arrivals in Maltese and Italian search-and-
rescue zones during 2023.⁴

The experience that the Grand Master’s Palace
offers its visitors has become even more engaging,
as nine halls which were previously kept closed are
now accessible to the public.
The newly opened halls mark another phase of the
restoration project at the Grand Master’s Palace. This
project, partly co-financed via the European Region-
al Development Fund, represents one of the most
significant investments in Malta’s cultural heritage. It
aligns directly with Heritage Malta’s mission to bring
heritage closer to the people and to reinforce Malta’s
national identity through meaningful cultural engage-
ment.
The project was driven by the urgent need to restore
and revitalise the palace, situated in the heart of
Valletta, a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Despite its
long-standing role as the central seat of power since it
was built in the 1570s – currently it still hosts the Office
of the President – the palace was lately mainly used
as offices by various government entities, provided
limited accessibility to visitors, and required extensive
upgrading and restoration as its historical interi-
ors and priceless artistic treasures were somewhat
shrouded in obscurity.
Through meticulous historical research, conservation
and strategic planning, the regeneration project has
transformed the Palace into a sustainable cultural
destination. Previously restricted areas are now open
to the public, offering an immersive historic house
experience that informs and inspires.
Some of the newly opened halls are adorned with
furniture and artefacts donated to Heritage Malta by
local collector Paul Zammit. Comprising one of the
largest and best donations that the national collection
has ever received, the donated items include exquisite
artefacts from the 16th to the 18th century, ranging
from intricately crafted furniture to paintings, chande-
liers, tapestries and damasks.
The newly opened halls are the Grand Master’s
Salotto/Studio, the Grand Master’s Bedroom, the
Chapel, the Cabinetto di Curiosità, the Conventual
Chaplain’s Room, the Treasury Office and Passage,
the French Hall, the Hall of the Order of St Michael
and St George, and the Hall dedicated to the Maltese
Parliament. Let’s take you for a whirlwind tour around
each one!
The Salotto/Studio was the Grand Master’s private
sitting room and study. During the British period, it
served as the office of the Governor’s Aide-de-camp.
It has been known as the Popes’ Room since the Or-
der’s period, due to its embellishment with portraits of
pontiffs. A fresco cycle by Leonello Spada depicts the
history of the Order of St John whilst still in Jerusalem.
During the Order’s rule, the Grand Master’s Bedroom
formed part of the palace’s summer quarters and
could be accessed from both the Grand Master’s
sitting room and a small passageway. Not visible un-
der the present early 18th century frieze adorning the
bedroom are the remains of an earlier frieze in fresco
showing various putti holding the coat of arms of
Grand Master Wignacourt (1601-1622) and emblems
of the Order of St John.
The Chapel was part of the Palace’s summer quarters
and was administered by the Order’s palace conven-
tual chaplains. Under British rule, it was converted
into an office for the Lieutenant-Governor and at
times served as a bedroom. Its present configuration
respects its original intended religious use.
The original use of the room now known as the
Cabinetto di Curiosità is not clear. It has been sug-
gested that it was the chapel’s sacristy and part of the
Order’s Treasury administration. As presently config-
ured, it hosts the Cabinet of Curiosities, which occu-
pied various locations around the Palace during both
the Order’s and British rule.
The Conventual Chaplain’s Room is believed to have
been used as the Chaplain’s bedroom under the Or-

der’s rule. The Conventual Chaplains were priests and
members of the Order of St John. They served in all
the Order’s churches around Malta and Gozo, includ-
ing all chapels within the Grand Master’s palaces, the
fort churches, and on the Order’s fleet.
The Treasury Office is located near the passage and
staircase that led to the Order’s Treasury and Coin

possessions.
During the Order’s rule, the French Hall was possibly
the quarters for the Grand Master’s entourage. During
the British period, it was likewise used for officers in
the Governor’s service. This room offers insight into
the turbulent years of the French Revolution, its impact
on the Order’s finances, the eventual ousting of the
Order from Malta by the French, the Maltese uprising
against the French, and the eventual British rule of
Malta.
The Hall of the Order of St Michael and St George
bears the name of an order instituted in 1818 in com-
memoration of the inclusion of Malta and the Ionian
Islands in the British Empire. Its setting up was spear-
headed by the then Governor of Malta, Sir Thomas
Maitland.
The room which briefly tells the history of the Maltese
Parliament was part of a larger hall up till 1715, when
this hall was split into a large hall and two smaller
rooms. Period photographs in the room show the two
chambers of the Maltese Parliament within the Grand
Master’s Palace. A small showcase displays items
related to the Maltese Parliament during the period it
was housed at the Palace.

IN recent years, Maltese architecture
has undergone a quiet yet profound
transformation, an evolution that is neither about
rejecting tradition but weaving it with a novel
approach into a living fabric that reflects Malta’s
unique culture, climate, and community spirit.
The Premju Emanuele Luigi Galizia 2024 – the
annual Chamber of Architects awards – as well
as the Planning Authority’s Malta Architecture
and Spatial Planning awards, have provided a vivid
showcase of this shift, with each award highlighting
a distinctive trend that is shaping the Maltese home
and its place in society.
The Premju’s jury’s motivations, far from being
mere formalities, tell the story of a profession
committed to creativity, heritage, sustainability,
and community impact. Architects in Malta
are responding to the challenges of dense
urban environments, changing lifestyles, and
environmental responsibilities, while finding ways to
make the home a centre of life and meaning.
“Malta’s vernacular is not just an aesthetic; it’s
a response to climate, culture and community,”
says architect André Pizzuto, president of Malta’s
Chamber of Architects. “Traditional Maltese
houses are built in local limestone, organised
around internal courtyards that provide light
and ventilation, and articulated with restrained
ornament – the traditional closed balcony, the
bowed gallarija, the thick walls and arches. They
embody what I like to conceive of as architectural
restraint: a conscious economy of means that
harnesses material, geometry and craftsmanship to
create comfort and dignity.”
Inseparable from their setting, with courtyards
open to the sky, façades modulating light and heat,
and spaces scaled for family rather than spectacle,
Pizzuto describes Maltese architecture as “a quiet
form of civic intelligence: it speaks to our history
and to a way of building that was attuned to place,
climate and social life. Any celebration of Maltese
architecture must start from this understanding that
architecture is not just about style but about how we
inhabit and care for our environment.”

By Matthew Vella
INthe constellation of nations that have
danced in orbit around the American
experiment, few seem more peripheral than
Malta: outpost of empire and British fortress,
wartime martyr, a diplomatic crossroads…
the casual observer is often surprised at
discovering this microstate at the edge of
Europe.
But to the careful eye, and perhaps for the
United States, ever young, ever far away, Malta
has, again and again, turned up at moments
of profound significance. And if history is
written not just by victors but by those who
remember, then Malta has earned its place
in America’s memory not with size, but with
steadfastness.

seem, began in revolution. In the late 18th

idea still trembling into being, half-metaphor,
half musket-smoke. Its allies were few, its navy
meagre, its future uncertain. Yet even then, a
current ran through the Mediterranean that
brought it into Maltese harbours.
“Even before the United States of America
came into existence there had been a
connection between it and Malta, albeit an
unusual one,” writes Sarah Wilkinson, curator
of the Museum of the Order of St John, in

During the War of Independence, around 1,800
individuals from Malta, including both Maltese
residents and members of the Knights of the
Order, joined the ranks of the French Navy to
support the emerging United States during its
struggle for independence.
The involvement of the Maltese proved pivotal
in securing victory. In 1781, in a significant turn
of events, Maltese sailors played a vital role in
aiding the French Navy to achieve victory over
the British in the Battle of the Chesapeake. This
triumph had far-reaching consequences as it
prevented the British forces from regrouping and
replenishing their resources.
As a result, the American army capitalised on this
advantage, compelling the British to surrender
and ultimately bringing an end to the war. The
Battle of the Chesapeake, rarely lionised in
schoolbooks, was in fact the beginning of the end
for British imperial designs in North America.
In 1783, Benjamin Franklin – minister, printer,
alchemist of public opinion – sent to Grand
Master Emmanuel de Rohan the Libertas
Americana, a medal struck in France, cast with
the ideal of freedom, and offered in gratitude. It
was a political gesture disguised as a gift.
Franklin, whose genius lay as much in diplomacy
as in electricity, was asking for more than thanks:
he asked that American ships be received in
Malta. And they were. De Rohan, in his reply,
spoke of liberty, of welcome, and of friendship
between nations not yet neighbours, but bound by
the shifting winds of trade and principle.
A maritime understanding was born then.
When the United States found itself at odds with
the corsairs of the Barbary Coast in the early 19th
century, its ships called on Malta for fresh water
and provisions. In 1796, with the appointment
of the first American consul to Malta, William
England, a consular thread was tied to Valletta,
a stony hub in a violent sea. America, emerging
into global relevance, had found a node. The

Malta survived. And in surviving, it helped turn

a conference room for empires.

independent.

Geography is inescapable.
The Mediterranean occupies a central place
in Europe’s neighbourhood, with direct
repercussions on security and stability.
The effectiveness of European political
engagement is therefore crucial. If the EU is
not motivated and ambitious to strengthen
and clarify its involvement, there is a
significant likelihood that external powers
will step in to fill any resulting void,
potentially shaping the region’s trajectory
in ways that may not align with European
interests.
Historically, the Mediterranean experienced
periods of relative peace under the Roman
Empire
(Pax Romana) and later British
dominance (
Pax Britannica), when the
region was considered a strategic asset.
Nowadays,
Pax Europea remains a mirage
and sovereignty rests with independent
Mediterranean states. Can the concept
of a
Pax Mediterranea be determined by
regional actors and their vision and political
will? Whether this is a realistic pursuit
or an optimistic ideal, warrants critical
reflection.
